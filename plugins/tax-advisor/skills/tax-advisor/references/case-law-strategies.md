# Case Law & Ruling Strategies Reference — Tax Year 2025

> **Purpose:** Curated case law, IRS acquiescences, revenue rulings, and private letter rulings that create planning opportunities differing from the "default" reading of the Internal Revenue Code. Each entry documents the taxpayer-favorable outcome and its practical application.

---

## Risk Level Framework for Case Law

When citing case law to support a tax strategy, assign risk based on the legal status of the authority:

| Authority Level | Risk Rating | Rationale |
|----------------|-------------|-----------|
| IRC statute + clear Treasury regulation | **Conservative** | Black-letter law, no ambiguity |
| IRS acquiescence (AOD) to court ruling | **Conservative** | IRS has formally agreed not to litigate further |
| Revenue Ruling (Rev. Rul.) | **Conservative** | Official IRS position, binding on IRS agents |
| Supreme Court ruling | **Conservative** | Binding nationwide |
| Circuit Court ruling (with IRS non-appeal) | **Conservative–Moderate** | Strong authority; note which circuit |
| Circuit Court ruling (without IRS acquiescence) | **Moderate** | Binding in that circuit only; IRS may litigate in others |
| Tax Court regular decision | **Moderate** | Persuasive but not binding; IRS may dispute in other cases |
| Tax Court memorandum decision | **Moderate–Aggressive** | Fact-specific, limited precedential value |
| Private Letter Ruling (PLR) | **Aggressive** | Cannot be cited as precedent (IRC §6110(k)(3)); shows IRS thinking only |
| IRS Chief Counsel Advice (CCA) | **Moderate** | Not binding but reveals IRS legal position |

## Mortgage Interest — Unmarried Co-Owners

### Voss v. Commissioner, 796 F.3d 1051 (9th Cir. 2015)

**Holding:** Unmarried co-owners of a qualified residence each get their own separate §163(h)(3) acquisition indebtedness limit. The debt limit is applied per taxpayer, not per residence.

**IRS Response:** Acquiescence — AOD 2016-02 (IRB 2016-02). The IRS formally agreed with the Ninth Circuit's holding and will follow it nationwide. This is not limited to the 9th Circuit.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** — IRS acquiescence makes this settled law.

**Practical Impact:**
- Under current law (TCJA/OBBBA), the acquisition indebtedness limit is $750,000 per taxpayer ($375,000 MFS)
- An unmarried couple co-owning a home effectively gets a combined $1,500,000 limit — double the $750,000 limit that applies to a married couple filing jointly
- A married couple filing jointly is limited to deducting interest on $750,000 of acquisition debt total, regardless of how many taxpayers are on the mortgage

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer is unmarried and co-owns a residence with a partner
- Taxpayer is considering marriage and has a large mortgage (marriage would reduce the combined limit)
- Mortgage exceeds $750,000 and the co-owners are not married
- Do **not** extend Voss to married-filing-separately limits; the statute supplies separate MFS caps

**Example:** Two unmarried partners buy a $1.4M home with a $1.12M mortgage (80% LTV). Each claims $560,000 of acquisition debt against their individual $750,000 limit — both are under the cap. If they were married filing jointly, only $750,000 of the $1.12M mortgage would qualify, losing the deduction on $370,000 of debt. At a 6.5% interest rate and 32% marginal bracket, the marriage penalty on this single issue is approximately $7,700/year in lost deductions.

**Citation:** Voss v. Commissioner, 796 F.3d 1051 (9th Cir. 2015), rev'g T.C. Memo 2012-198; AOD 2016-02.

## Home Office — Allocation Methods

### Prop. Reg. §1.280A-2(i) — Allocation of Expenses

**Authority:** Proposed Treasury Regulation §1.280A-2(i)(5), Example 4. Though never finalized, the IRS has not withdrawn it, and the Tax Court has applied it in multiple cases.

**Holding/Principle:** A qualifying taxpayer using the actual-expense method allocates indirect home expenses between business and personal use under §280A and Form 8829 rules. A reasonable method may use square footage or rooms when rooms are comparable.

**Key Case:**
- **Scott v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 1996-72:** Addresses allocation of home expenses in a fact-specific setting. Verify the current Form 8829 instructions before relying on the case.

**Risk Level:** **Moderate** — Allocation is established, but the proposed regulation and memorandum decision are not authority for a double deduction.

**Practical Impact:**
- Allocate each expense once. The business portion may be deductible through Form 8829/Schedule C; the personal portion may be deductible on Schedule A only if it independently qualifies.
- Never say mortgage interest or property tax is “deducted twice.” The business and personal pieces cannot exceed the supported total expense.
- Compare the actual and simplified methods using the taxpayer's records. The simplified method has different rules and may preserve personal itemized deductions; do not assume the actual method always wins.

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer has a home office and itemizes (or is close to the itemized threshold)
- Self-employed taxpayer with a large home office relative to total home size
- Taxpayer uses the simplified method but has significant mortgage interest/property tax

## Trade or Business Status — Full-Time Gambling

### Commissioner v. Groetzinger, 480 U.S. 23 (1987)

**Holding:** The Supreme Court held that a full-time gambler who gambles with regularity, continuity, and with the primary purpose of earning a livelihood is engaged in a "trade or business" under §162, not merely an "activity engaged in for profit" under §183 (hobby loss). This entitles the taxpayer to deduct gambling losses as business expenses on Schedule C rather than as itemized deductions on Schedule A.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** — Supreme Court decision, fully binding.

**Practical Impact:**
- Trade or business status for gambling allows deduction of losses as business expenses (Schedule C), which reduces AGI — unlike Schedule A deductions, which do not
- Business expenses can offset gambling income dollar-for-dollar without the itemization requirement
- Gamblers in a trade or business can also deduct related expenses: travel, lodging, entry fees, publications, equipment, and professional services
- The §461(l) excess business loss limitation ($305,000 single / $610,000 MFJ for 2025) applies

**Key Test (Groetzinger factors):**
1. Gambling is pursued full-time (or substantially full-time)
2. In good faith, with regularity and continuity
3. With the primary objective of earning a livelihood
4. Not merely as a hobby or recreational activity

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer has substantial gambling income and losses
- Professional poker players, sports bettors, or day traders (note: day trading in securities has its own §475(f) mark-to-market election)
- Taxpayer asks about hobby loss rules in the context of gambling

**Citation:** Commissioner v. Groetzinger, 480 U.S. 23, 107 S.Ct. 980 (1987).

## Tax Home Definition — Dual Residences

### Hantzis v. Commissioner, 638 F.2d 248 (1st Cir. 1981)

**Holding:** A taxpayer's "tax home" for purposes of the travel expense deduction under §162(a)(2) is the taxpayer's principal place of business, not their personal residence. When a taxpayer maintains a permanent residence in one city but works temporarily in another, travel expenses (including lodging) at the temporary work location are deductible only if the taxpayer has a business reason for maintaining the residence in the first city.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** — First Circuit decision, widely cited and followed by other circuits and the Tax Court.

**Practical Impact:**
- Taxpayers with business reasons for maintaining two locations (e.g., two offices, two business activities) can deduct travel expenses at the secondary location
- Taxpayers who temporarily work away from their tax home (assignments expected to last one year or less) can deduct travel, lodging, and 50% of meals
- The key is establishing a business connection to the "home" location — personal convenience alone is insufficient

**Key Principles:**
1. Tax home = principal place of business (or if no principal place, the regular place of abode)
2. Temporary vs. indefinite: assignments expected to last more than one year are treated as indefinite, and the new location becomes the tax home
3. Three-factor test from Rev. Rul. 93-86: (a) business activity in each location, (b) duplication of living expenses, (c) personal ties to claimed tax home

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer works in a different city from their home
- Remote workers who travel to employer's office periodically
- Taxpayers with multiple business locations
- Traveling nurses, consultants, construction workers, or other temporary assignments
- Taxpayer asks about deducting travel or maintaining two residences

**Citation:** Hantzis v. Commissioner, 638 F.2d 248 (1st Cir. 1981), rev'g T.C. Memo 1979-299.

## S-Corp Reasonable Compensation — Benchmarks

### Key Cases Establishing Reasonable Compensation Standards

Multiple Tax Court decisions have established the framework for determining "reasonable compensation" for S-Corp officer-shareholders, which directly affects self-employment tax liability.

**Watson v. United States, 668 F.3d 1008 (8th Cir. 2012)**

**Holding:** The Eighth Circuit upheld the district court's determination that an S-Corp accounting firm partner's salary of $24,000 on $200,000+ of distributions was unreasonably low. The court endorsed a multi-factor analysis and approved the IRS's recharacterization of a portion of distributions as wages subject to employment tax.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative–Moderate** — Circuit court decision. The specific salary amount is fact-dependent, but the multi-factor test is widely accepted.

**Radtke v. United States, 895 F.2d 1196 (7th Cir. 1990)**

**Holding:** An S-Corp attorney who paid himself zero salary but took all income as distributions had 100% of his distributions recharacterized as wages. The court held that services rendered to the corporation require reasonable compensation.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** — Clear bright line: zero salary is never reasonable when the shareholder performs services.

**Joly v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 1998-361**

**Holding:** The Tax Court established that reasonable compensation should be determined by reference to what comparable enterprises pay for similar services, considering factors including training, experience, duties, time devoted, and the corporation's dividend history.

**Multi-Factor Test (consolidated from case law):**

1. **Training and experience** of the employee
2. **Duties and responsibilities** performed
3. **Time and effort devoted** to the business
4. **Comparable compensation** in similar enterprises for similar services
5. **Size and complexity** of the business
6. **Economic conditions** generally and locally
7. **Salary vs. distribution history** of the corporation
8. **Compensation agreements** and their arm's-length nature
9. **Use of a compensation formula** (e.g., percentage of revenue)

**Practical Impact:**
- Distributions above reasonable compensation may avoid some employment tax, but the savings depend on wage-base and Medicare rules; do not apply a flat 15.3% to every dollar
- There is **no 60/40 or other percentage safe harbor**. A salary equal to 60% of profit can still be unreasonable on the facts
- Comparable replacement compensation is evidence, not a standalone safe harbor
- The IRS targets S-Corps with very low officer compensation relative to revenue and distributions
- Document the compensation analysis annually — keep a memo explaining how the salary was determined

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer has an S-Corp or is considering S-Corp election
- S-Corp officer salary appears disproportionately low relative to distributions
- Taxpayer asks "how much salary should I take from my S-Corp?"
- Taxpayer has S-Corp with $0 officer compensation (immediate red flag)

**Citations:** Watson v. United States, 668 F.3d 1008 (8th Cir. 2012); Radtke v. United States, 895 F.2d 1196 (7th Cir. 1990); Joly v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 1998-361.

## Qualified Small Business Stock — Stacking with Trusts

### IRC §1202 and PLR 201436001

**Statute:** IRC §1202 can exclude qualifying gain subject to per-issuer limits, holding periods, active-business tests, and gross-asset limits. OBBBA changed holding-period percentages and the gross-asset threshold for certain stock acquired after its effective date. Verify the acquisition date and current statute rather than applying the legacy $50M/five-year rules to every 2025 acquisition.

**PLR 201436001 — Trust Stacking**

**Authority:** Private Letter Ruling — cannot be cited as precedent, but reveals IRS thinking on the issue.

**Principle:** Section 1202's limitation and carryover rules can apply separately to distinct taxpayers, and §1202(h) addresses certain gifts. Multiple non-grantor trusts may produce separate limits only if each trust is respected and every statutory requirement is met. Do not present a multiplication example as an approved result.

**Risk Level:** **Aggressive** — PLR is non-precedential. The IRS has not formally endorsed trust stacking in published guidance. Several tax practitioners advise it, but the IRS could challenge the trusts if they lack economic substance or independent purpose beyond tax avoidance.

**Key Requirements:**
1. Each trust must be a separate taxpayer (irrevocable, non-grantor trust)
2. Gifts of QSBS must be completed gifts (not revocable or retained interests)
3. Each trust's holding period includes the donor's holding period (tacking applies)
4. The trusts should have independent purposes (different beneficiaries, different terms)
5. Step transaction doctrine risk: gifts made shortly before a known sale are more vulnerable

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer holds QSBS with unrealized gain exceeding $10M
- Startup founder approaching a liquidity event (acquisition, IPO)
- Taxpayer asks about maximizing §1202 exclusion
- Any C-Corp stock held more than 5 years with significant appreciation

**Citation:** IRC §1202; PLR 201436001 (2014).

## Charitable Contributions — Timing and Delivery

### Estate/Trust Case Law on Contribution Timing

**Key Principle:** A charitable contribution is deductible in the year of delivery, not the year of payment or the year the charity deposits the check. Several cases have addressed the timing rules.

**Rev. Rul. 78-38 — Check Delivery Rule**

**Authority:** Revenue Ruling — binding on IRS.

**Holding:** A charitable contribution made by check is deductible in the year the check is delivered or mailed to the charity, provided the check clears in due course. The taxpayer does not need to wait until the charity deposits or cashes the check.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** — Revenue Ruling, well-established.

**Practical Impact:**
- A check mailed on December 31 is deductible in the current tax year even if the charity doesn't deposit it until January
- Credit card donations are deductible when charged, not when the credit card bill is paid
- Wire transfers are deductible when sent
- Pledges (promises to pay) are NOT deductible until actually paid — except for accrual-method taxpayers

**Estate of Stotler v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 1987-275**

**Holding:** Delivery controls, but completion depends on the transfer method and control relinquished. For an electronic brokerage transfer, do not assume the initiation date controls; obtain the charity/custodian receipt showing when the shares entered the charity's account and apply current IRS guidance.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** — Well-established timing rule.

**Practical Impact:**
- For year-end appreciated stock donations: initiate the transfer by mid-December to ensure completion by December 31 (brokerage transfers can take 3-7 business days)
- If donating through a DAF, the contribution is complete when the DAF receives the stock, not when grants are made from the DAF
- Document the FMV on the delivery date with a market quote

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Year-end tax planning (November-December)
- Large charitable contribution being timed across tax years
- Donating appreciated stock or other non-cash property
- Bunching strategy implementation
- DAF contribution timing

**Citations:** Rev. Rul. 78-38, 1978-1 C.B. 68; Estate of Stotler v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 1987-275.

## Day Trading — Mark-to-Market Election

### IRC §475(f) and Chen v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2004-132

**Statute:** IRC §475(f) allows a "trader in securities" to elect mark-to-market accounting, which treats all securities held at year-end as sold at FMV on the last business day of the year. Gains and losses are ordinary (not capital), eliminating the $3,000 capital loss limitation and wash sale concerns.

**Chen v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2004-132**

**Holding:** Established key factors for qualifying as a "trader" (versus "investor") under §475(f): the taxpayer must seek to profit from daily market movements (not long-term appreciation), trading must be frequent and substantial, and the taxpayer must devote substantial time to the activity.

**Risk Level:** **Moderate** — The election itself is conservative (statutory), but qualifying as a "trader" is fact-intensive. The IRS frequently challenges trader status.

**Trader vs. Investor Test (from case law):**
1. **Frequency and volume** of trades (hundreds to thousands per year)
2. **Holding periods** are short (days to weeks, not months to years)
3. **Time devoted** to trading is substantial (approaching full-time)
4. **Intent** is to profit from daily market movements, not dividends or long-term appreciation
5. **Trading** is the taxpayer's primary income-producing activity or a significant one

**Practical Impact:**
- Ordinary loss treatment: eliminates the $3,000/year capital loss limitation — a trader with $100,000 in losses can deduct the full amount against other income
- No wash sale rule: mark-to-market eliminates wash sale concerns entirely
- Self-employment tax: mark-to-market gains/losses are generally NOT subject to SE tax (they are not from a trade or business for SE tax purposes under most circuit court interpretations)
- Election must be made by the due date of the prior year's return (or by April 15 for a new taxpayer beginning trading)
- The election is irrevocable without IRS consent

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer is an active day trader or swing trader
- Taxpayer has large capital losses exceeding the $3,000 annual deduction limit
- Taxpayer asks about wash sale rule problems from frequent trading
- Crypto traders (note: §475(f) applies to "securities" and "commodities" — crypto classification is evolving)

**Citation:** IRC §475(f); Chen v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2004-132; Endicott v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2013-199.

## Passive Activity Losses — Short-Term Rental Exception

### IRC §469(c)(7) and Reg. §1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)

**Authority:** Statute and Treasury Regulation — binding.

**Principle:** Rental activities are generally passive under §469, meaning losses can only offset other passive income. However, Reg. §1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii) provides that a rental activity is NOT treated as a rental activity (and therefore is not automatically passive) if the average customer use period is 7 days or less. If the taxpayer also materially participates, the losses become non-passive and can offset wages, business income, and other active income.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** — Statutory exception with clear regulatory guidance. Widely used and well-established.

**Practical Impact:**
- Short-term rental properties (Airbnb, VRBO) with average stays of 7 days or less escape the passive activity rules entirely if the taxpayer materially participates
- Combined with cost segregation and bonus depreciation, this can create large "paper losses" that offset W-2 income in early years
- Material participation requires meeting one of seven tests under §469(h) — most commonly: 500+ hours per year, or substantially all participation is by the taxpayer
- This is NOT the same as Real Estate Professional Status (REPS), which has its own requirements under §469(c)(7)

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer owns or is considering short-term rental property
- Taxpayer asks about passive losses from rental property
- High-income W-2 earner looking for loss deductions against salary
- Taxpayer mentions Airbnb, VRBO, or vacation rental income/losses

**Citation:** IRC §469(c)(7); Reg. §1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii); IRC §469(h).

## Net Unrealized Appreciation (NUA) — Employer Stock

### IRC §402(e)(4) and Rev. Rul. 2002-1

**Statute:** IRC §402(e)(4) allows participants with employer stock in a qualified plan (401(k), ESOP) to take a lump-sum distribution of the stock and pay ordinary income tax only on the cost basis of the stock within the plan. The "net unrealized appreciation" (the difference between basis and FMV at distribution) is not taxed until the stock is sold, and then at long-term capital gains rates regardless of the holding period after distribution.

**Rev. Rul. 2002-1**

**Authority:** Revenue Ruling — binding.

**Holding:** Confirmed that NUA treatment applies to the entire amount of appreciation at the time of distribution, and any additional appreciation after distribution receives capital gains treatment based on the actual post-distribution holding period.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** — Statutory provision with clear IRS guidance.

**Practical Impact:**
- If employer stock has a low cost basis and significant appreciation, NUA can save substantial taxes compared to rolling the stock into an IRA (where all distributions are taxed as ordinary income)
- Example: $50,000 basis, $500,000 FMV = $450,000 NUA. Without NUA: rolling to IRA and withdrawing = $450,000 × 37% = $166,500 tax. With NUA: $50,000 × 37% = $18,500 ordinary tax + $450,000 × 20% = $90,000 LTCG tax = $108,500 total. **Savings: $58,000.**
- Must be a "lump-sum distribution" — the entire vested balance must be distributed in one tax year
- Triggering event required: separation from service (any age), death, disability, or attaining age 59½
- Can split: take employer stock as NUA distribution, roll everything else to an IRA

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer is leaving a job where they hold significant employer stock in 401(k)
- Taxpayer has highly appreciated employer stock in a retirement plan
- Taxpayer is retiring and deciding how to handle employer stock
- ESOP participants receiving distributions

**Citation:** IRC §402(e)(4); Rev. Rul. 2002-1, 2002-1 C.B. 784.

## Conservation Easements — Legitimate vs. Abusive

### IRC §170(h), §170(h)(7), and IRS Listed-Transaction Guidance

**Statute:** IRC §170(h) permits a deduction for a qualified conservation contribution only when detailed ownership, conservation-purpose, perpetuity, substantiation, and valuation rules are met. Congress added §170(h)(7), which disallows many pass-through contributions when the claimed amount exceeds the statutory basis ratio, subject to exceptions.

**Hewitt caution:** **Hewitt v. Commissioner, 21 F.4th 1336 (11th Cir. 2021)** was a taxpayer-favorable Administrative Procedure Act decision concerning the extinguishment-proceeds regulation. It did **not** generally bless syndicated transactions or inflated appraisals and should not be cited for that proposition.

**IRS guidance:** Notice 2017-10 identified specified syndicated conservation easement transactions as transactions of interest; later regulations and statutory amendments must be checked for the contribution year.

**Risk Level:** Even a direct landowner donation is **Moderate–Aggressive** because perpetuity, appraisal, baseline documentation, and deed language are frequent litigation issues. Promoted pass-through transactions with outsized deduction-to-investment ratios are **Extremely Aggressive / Potentially Disallowed by Statute**.

**Pattern Triggers:** If a taxpayer owns conservation land or is offered a promoted easement transaction, require specialist counsel and an independent qualified appraiser before transfer. Do not market an easement as an ordinary tax strategy.

**Citations:** IRC §170(h), including §170(h)(7); Hewitt v. Commissioner, 21 F.4th 1336 (11th Cir. 2021); Notice 2017-10. Verify current listed-transaction regulations and circuit law.

## Hobby Loss Safe Harbor — Presumption of Profit

### IRC §183(d) and Helmick v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2009-220

**Statute:** Under the hobby loss rules (§183), an activity is presumed to be engaged in for profit if it produces a net profit in 3 of the last 5 tax years (2 of 7 for horse breeding/racing/showing). If the presumption is met, the burden shifts to the IRS to prove the activity is a hobby.

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** (if safe harbor is met) / **Moderate** (if relying on nine-factor test without meeting safe harbor).

**Nine-Factor Test (Reg. §1.183-2(b)):**
1. Manner in which the taxpayer carries on the activity (businesslike?)
2. Expertise of the taxpayer or advisors
3. Time and effort expended
4. Expectation that assets may appreciate
5. Success in similar activities
6. History of income or losses
7. Amount of occasional profits
8. Financial status of the taxpayer
9. Elements of personal pleasure or recreation

**Practical Impact:**
- Side businesses, farming operations, art activities, horse breeding, and other activities with mixed personal/profit motives must navigate these rules
- Strategy: structure the activity to meet the 3-of-5-year safe harbor. If losses are expected in early years, elect under §183(e) to postpone the determination until the end of the 5-year period
- Maintain meticulous records: separate bank accounts, business plans, time logs, profit projections, and evidence of expertise

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer has a side business or activity that consistently generates losses
- Taxpayer asks about deducting losses from farming, art, writing, horse activities, or other potentially hobby-like activities
- IRS has challenged an activity as a hobby

**Citation:** IRC §183; Reg. §1.183-2(b); Helmick v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2009-220.

## Qualified Opportunity Zone — Basis Step-Up

### IRC §1400Z-2 and Reg. §1.1400Z2(b)-1

**Statute:** IRC §1400Z-2 (added by TCJA 2017) allows taxpayers to defer and potentially reduce capital gains by investing in Qualified Opportunity Zone Funds (QOFs). While the original 10% and 15% basis step-ups for holding 5 and 7 years have expired (those required investment by 2019/2021), the permanent benefit remains.

**Permanent Benefit (Still Available):**
- If a QOF investment is held for **10 or more years**, the taxpayer may elect to increase the basis of the QOF interest to its FMV at the time of sale, effectively **eliminating all appreciation** in the QOF investment from taxation
- The original deferred gain is recognized no later than December 31, 2026 (regardless of whether the QOF interest is sold)

**Risk Level:** **Conservative** — Statutory provision with extensive final regulations. However, compliance requirements are detailed and penalties for failure to maintain QOF status are significant.

**Practical Impact:**
- New investments today: the deferral benefit is limited (deferred gain is recognized by 12/31/2026 at the latest), but the 10-year basis step-up on appreciation within the QOF is extremely valuable for long-term investors
- Best use case now: investing capital gains into QOF real estate or business development projects expected to appreciate significantly over 10+ years
- The appreciation exclusion has no cap — a $1M QOF investment that grows to $5M results in $4M of tax-free appreciation if held 10+ years

**Pattern Triggers — Surface This When:**
- Taxpayer has large capital gains and a long time horizon
- Taxpayer asks about opportunity zones or QOFs
- Real estate investor looking for tax-advantaged development opportunities
- Taxpayer is evaluating what to do with proceeds from a business sale

**Citation:** IRC §1400Z-2; Reg. §1.1400Z2(a)-1; Reg. §1.1400Z2(b)-1.

## How to Use This Reference

### For the Tax Situation Analyzer

When analyzing a user's situation, cross-reference their profile against the **Pattern Triggers** in each case above. If a match is found, proactively surface the case law and explain the planning opportunity — even if the user didn't ask about it. Frame the recommendation with:
1. The case/ruling and its holding
2. Why it applies to their specific situation
3. The estimated dollar impact
4. The risk level based on authority type
5. Implementation steps

### For the Tax Strategy Explorer

When brainstorming creative strategies, search this reference and the knowledge base for supporting case law. Every moderate or aggressive strategy should cite:
1. The IRC section authorizing the strategy
2. Any court cases that have ruled favorably
3. Whether the IRS acquiesced or continues to litigate
4. The risk level based on the authority framework above

### For the Tax Document Reviewer

When reviewing documents, check for fact patterns that match case law opportunities. For example:
- If the mortgage exceeds $750K and the taxpayer is unmarried with a co-owner → flag Voss
- If there's employer stock in a 401(k) and the taxpayer is separating from service → flag NUA
- If there's a Schedule C home office using the simplified method → flag the regular method with allocation case law
